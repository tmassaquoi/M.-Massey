# Foundations of Youth Development & Mentoring
## A Comprehensive Training Program Checklist for Organizational Leaders

**Designed for:** Organizational leaders, program directors, and aspiring youth development practitioners
**Purpose:** Foundational training to equip leaders with the knowledge, frameworks, and tools to develop, lead, and sustain high-quality youth development and mentoring programming
**Delivery Model:** Leaders complete this training, then receive facilitation support to train volunteers and community stakeholders in their respective modules

---

## Theoretical & Philosophical Grounding

This training program integrates five interconnected frameworks:

| Framework | Core Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Positive Youth Development (PYD)** | Strengths-based lens; youth as assets, not problems to fix |
| **Search Institute's Developmental Assets** | 40 building blocks (internal & external) young people need to thrive |
| **Karen Pittman's Youth Development Ecosystems** | "Problem-free is not fully prepared" — ecosystems of adults, settings, and supports |
| **Elements of Effective Practice in Mentoring (EEPM)** | Quality standards for mentoring program design and management |
| **Adrienne Maree Brown's Emergent Strategy** | Adaptive, relational, and justice-centered organizational practice |

# MODULE 1: FOUNDATIONS — The Why, What, and How of Youth Development

*For Organizational Leaders | Prerequisite to all other modules*

---

## Section 1.1 — Positive Youth Development Framework

### Conceptual Understanding
- [ ] Define Positive Youth Development (PYD) and distinguish it from deficit-based approaches
- [ ] Articulate the Five Cs of PYD: Competence, Confidence, Connection, Character, and Caring/Compassion
- [ ] Explain how PYD shifts the question from "what's wrong with young people?" to "what do young people need to thrive?"
- [ ] Identify the difference between youth programming and youth development
- [ ] Describe how PYD applies across race, ethnicity, culture, gender identity, ability, and socioeconomic context

### Organizational Application
- [ ] Audit existing programs or program concepts through a PYD lens
- [ ] Identify language and communication patterns in your organization that reflect (or contradict) a strengths-based philosophy
- [ ] Articulate how PYD will be embedded in your organization's mission, vision, and values
- [ ] Develop at least three concrete examples of how PYD will show up in day-to-day programming

---

## Section 1.2 — Search Institute's 40 Developmental Assets

### Conceptual Understanding
- [ ] Distinguish between External Assets and Internal Assets
- [ ] Name and describe the four categories of **External Assets**:
  - [ ] Support (family support, positive family communication, other adult relationships, caring neighborhood, caring school climate, parent involvement in schooling)
  - [ ] Empowerment (community values youth, youth as resources, service to others, safety)
  - [ ] Boundaries & Expectations (family boundaries, school boundaries, neighborhood boundaries, adult role models, positive peer influence, high expectations)
  - [ ] Constructive Use of Time (creative activities, youth programs, religious community, time at home)
- [ ] Name and describe the four categories of **Internal Assets**:
  - [ ] Commitment to Learning (achievement motivation, school engagement, homework, bonding to school, reading for pleasure)
  - [ ] Positive Values (caring, equality and social justice, integrity, honesty, responsibility, restraint)
  - [ ] Social Competencies (planning and decision-making, interpersonal competence, cultural competence, resistance skills, peaceful conflict resolution)
  - [ ] Positive Identity (personal power, self-esteem, sense of purpose, positive view of personal future)
- [ ] Explain the research basis: young people with more assets are more likely to thrive and less likely to engage in high-risk behaviors

### Organizational Application
- [ ] Map your existing (or planned) programming to specific developmental assets
- [ ] Identify which assets your programming currently builds and which are gaps
- [ ] Develop a plan to intentionally build at least 10 assets across your programming
- [ ] Create a framework for how staff and volunteers will discuss assets with youth and families
- [ ] Identify community partners who can help strengthen assets you cannot directly provide

---

## Section 1.3 — Karen Pittman's Youth Development Ecosystems

### Conceptual Understanding
- [ ] Explain the core argument: "Problem-free is not fully prepared, and fully prepared is not fully engaged"
- [ ] Describe the five developmental domains Pittman identifies:
  - [ ] Physical health and well-being
  - [ ] Intellectual/cognitive development
  - [ ] Psychological and emotional development
  - [ ] Social development
  - [ ] Moral and civic development
- [ ] Define a **youth development ecosystem**: the full range of people, places, institutions, and opportunities that shape a young person's growth
- [ ] Distinguish between access, quality, and equity in youth development ecosystems
- [ ] Explain how race, class, geography, and systemic barriers create ecosystem gaps for young people
- [ ] Identify the roles of family, school, community organizations, faith institutions, peers, and media in the ecosystem

### Organizational Application
- [ ] Map the youth development ecosystem in the specific community you serve
- [ ] Identify where your organization fits in the ecosystem and what role you play
- [ ] Identify ecosystem gaps your programming can address
- [ ] Document community partnerships that can help build a more complete ecosystem for youth
- [ ] Develop a strategy for how your organization will connect youth to other ecosystem supports

---

## Section 1.4 — Adrienne Maree Brown's Emergent Strategy

### Conceptual Understanding
- [ ] Explain the core premise: transformation happens through relationships, adaptation, and aligned action
- [ ] Describe the six principles of Emergent Strategy:
  - [ ] **Fractals:** Small is good, small is all — the shape of your work at the smallest level should reflect your values at every level
  - [ ] **Adaptive:** Conditions change; be willing to change with them rather than cling to old plans
  - [ ] **Interdependence:** We need each other; no organization or person thrives in isolation
  - [ ] **Decentralization:** Distribute leadership and trust the people closest to the work
  - [ ] **Nonlinear:** Change is not always visible or linear — trust the process
  - [ ] **Creating More Possibilities:** Orient toward abundance, not scarcity
- [ ] Explain the concept of "moving at the speed of trust"
- [ ] Connect Emergent Strategy principles to anti-oppressive, justice-centered youth work

### Organizational Application
- [ ] Evaluate your organization's current culture through an Emergent Strategy lens
- [ ] Identify at least two ways your programming design could be more adaptive and responsive
- [ ] Develop a plan for distributing leadership among staff, volunteers, and youth
- [ ] Practice the fractal question: "Does the way we treat staff reflect the values we want to model with youth?"
- [ ] Build a feedback loop where youth, volunteers, and families can influence program direction

---

## Section 1.5 — Integrating the Frameworks

- [ ] Draw connections between all five frameworks — identify where they reinforce each other
- [ ] Articulate a unified organizational philosophy that integrates PYD, developmental assets, ecosystem thinking, EEPM, and Emergent Strategy
- [ ] Develop an "organizational north star" statement that guides programming decisions
- [ ] Practice applying all five frameworks to a real or hypothetical programming scenario
- [ ] Create a reference tool (one-pager, visual, or card set) summarizing all frameworks for staff and volunteers

# MODULE 2: YOUTH ENGAGEMENT & RELATIONSHIP BUILDING

*For Organizational Leaders | Prepares leaders to train and facilitate with volunteers*

---

## Section 2.1 — Foundational Principles of Youth Engagement

- [ ] Define genuine youth engagement and distinguish it from token participation or compliance
- [ ] Identify the spectrum of youth participation (from informing to youth-led decision-making)
- [ ] Explain why developmental stage matters — how engagement looks different for younger adolescents vs. older teens vs. young adults
- [ ] Identify how race, gender, disability, and other identities shape how youth experience and respond to engagement efforts
- [ ] Articulate the neuroscience of adolescent development relevant to engagement and relationship (brain development, risk-taking, identity formation)

## Section 2.2 — Creating Conditions for Youth Engagement

- [ ] Design programming spaces that are physically and emotionally safe for youth
- [ ] Develop community agreements with youth rather than imposing rules
- [ ] Identify how to build in choice, voice, and agency for youth within structured programming
- [ ] Create rituals of welcome and belonging (how youth are greeted, celebrated, seen)
- [ ] Develop strategies for re-engaging youth who disengage or become inconsistent

## Section 2.3 — Relationship Building as a Core Practice

- [ ] Define the difference between a transactional interaction and a developmental relationship
- [ ] Identify the five elements of developmental relationships from Search Institute:
  - [ ] Express Care (be dependable, listen, believe in the young person)
  - [ ] Challenge Growth (expect the best, stretch, hold accountable)
  - [ ] Provide Support (navigate, empower, advocate)
  - [ ] Share Power (respect, include, collaborate)
  - [ ] Expand Possibilities (inspire, broaden horizons, connect)
- [ ] Practice relationship-building behaviors: active listening, curiosity, appropriate self-disclosure, consistency, and follow-through
- [ ] Recognize the role of cultural humility in cross-cultural relationships with youth
- [ ] Develop a relationship assessment tool to help staff and volunteers gauge relationship health
- [ ] Establish protocols for what healthy adult-youth relationships look like (and what they don't)

## Section 2.4 — Youth Voice, Leadership, and Decision-Making

- [ ] Create structured opportunities for youth to shape programming (youth advisory boards, feedback sessions, design teams)
- [ ] Develop an age-appropriate leadership pipeline for youth within your organization
- [ ] Practice facilitation techniques that center youth voice in group settings
- [ ] Train on how to respond to hard truths youth share without becoming defensive
- [ ] Document how youth input has changed or shaped programming (accountability and modeling)

## Section 2.5 — Trauma-Informed Youth Engagement

- [ ] Define trauma-informed care in the context of youth programming
- [ ] Recognize signs that a young person may be experiencing trauma or stress
- [ ] Understand the impact of systemic and community-level trauma (poverty, racism, community violence)
- [ ] Apply the four Rs of trauma-informed practice: Realize, Recognize, Respond, Resist Re-traumatization
- [ ] Develop referral protocols for when youth need support beyond your organization's capacity

# MODULE 3: VOLUNTEER ENGAGEMENT & DEVELOPMENT

*For Organizational Leaders | Prepares leaders to train and facilitate with volunteers*

---

## Section 3.1 — Foundations of Effective Mentoring (EEPM Alignment)

- [ ] Articulate the purpose and value of mentoring within a youth development ecosystem
- [ ] Introduce the **Elements of Effective Practice in Mentoring (EEPM)** and its six core elements:
  - [ ] Recruitment
  - [ ] Screening
  - [ ] Training
  - [ ] Matching
  - [ ] Monitoring & Support
  - [ ] Closure
- [ ] Explain how EEPM standards protect youth, mentors, and organizations
- [ ] Identify the difference between natural/informal mentoring and structured mentoring
- [ ] Connect mentoring to developmental assets — which assets does mentoring most directly build?

## Section 3.2 — Volunteer Recruitment (EEPM: Recruitment)

- [ ] Develop a clear profile of the volunteer you need based on your youth population and programming goals
- [ ] Create inclusive recruitment materials that reflect the diversity of your community
- [ ] Develop strategies for recruiting volunteers who reflect the racial, cultural, and linguistic backgrounds of youth served
- [ ] Identify high-trust recruitment channels (faith communities, HBCUs, community organizations, employers)
- [ ] Develop a screening and intake process that is welcoming and informative
- [ ] Set clear expectations for volunteer time commitment, roles, and responsibilities upfront

## Section 3.3 — Volunteer Screening (EEPM: Screening)

- [ ] Establish non-negotiable screening requirements:
  - [ ] Written application
  - [ ] In-depth interview
  - [ ] Reference checks (at least two)
  - [ ] Criminal background check (per EEPM standards)
- [ ] Develop interview protocols that assess motivation, values alignment, cultural competence, and reliability
- [ ] Create a decision-making rubric for evaluating applicants
- [ ] Develop a respectful process for declining applicants who are not a good fit
- [ ] Document all screening decisions and rationale

## Section 3.4 — Volunteer Training (EEPM: Training)

### Pre-Match Training (Before Volunteers Are Matched)
- [ ] Orient volunteers to your mission, values, and the youth development frameworks guiding your work
- [ ] Train on PYD and developmental assets — help volunteers see youth as whole people with strengths
- [ ] Train on the five elements of developmental relationships
- [ ] Set clear expectations for appropriate and inappropriate interactions (boundaries training)
- [ ] Review child protection policies, mandatory reporting, and confidentiality
- [ ] Train on cultural humility and how to navigate across difference with youth
- [ ] Practice relationship-building scenarios through role-play

### Ongoing Training (After Match)
- [ ] Establish a regular training calendar for volunteers (monthly or quarterly)
- [ ] Offer training on topics responsive to what volunteers are experiencing in their matches
- [ ] Create peer learning opportunities — volunteers learning from each other
- [ ] Connect training to Emergent Strategy: volunteers are part of a learning community, not just a delivery mechanism

## Section 3.5 — Matching (EEPM: Matching)

- [ ] Develop a matching criteria framework based on youth and volunteer information
- [ ] Consider: shared interests, geographic proximity, schedule compatibility, cultural background, gender identity preferences, language
- [ ] Consult with parents/caregivers and (age-appropriately) with youth in the matching process
- [ ] Conduct a formal match introduction meeting
- [ ] Set initial match goals and expectations
- [ ] Create documentation for the match and establish a monitoring timeline

## Section 3.6 — Monitoring & Support (EEPM: Monitoring & Support)

- [ ] Establish a regular check-in schedule for each match (at minimum monthly contact with mentor and youth)
- [ ] Develop a simple match quality survey or call protocol
- [ ] Train staff on how to identify signs of match difficulty or risk
- [ ] Create a process for addressing match challenges early — don't wait until the match is in crisis
- [ ] Provide mentors with ongoing access to a support person/coordinator
- [ ] Document all monitoring contacts and match milestones
- [ ] Celebrate match milestones to reinforce commitment and motivation

## Section 3.7 — Closure (EEPM: Closure)

- [ ] Establish a minimum match length commitment (EEPM recommends at least 12 months)
- [ ] Develop a protocol for planned closure: celebrate the relationship, acknowledge growth, discuss how to stay connected appropriately
- [ ] Develop a protocol for unplanned/early closure that minimizes harm to youth
- [ ] Create a re-matching process for youth whose mentor closes early
- [ ] Conduct exit interviews with mentors and youth/families after closure
- [ ] Use closure data to inform program improvement

## Section 3.8 — Volunteer Sustainability (Emergent Strategy Integration)

- [ ] Cultivate a volunteer community, not just a volunteer roster — connection builds retention
- [ ] Celebrate and publicly recognize volunteer contributions
- [ ] Create pathways for volunteers to grow into larger roles (group facilitators, trainers, advisory board members)
- [ ] Practice decentralized leadership — experienced volunteers mentor newer ones
- [ ] Develop a feedback mechanism that allows volunteers to shape program practices
- [ ] Apply the fractal principle: the way you treat volunteers is the way they will treat youth

# MODULE 4: PARENT & CAREGIVER ENGAGEMENT

*For Organizational Leaders | Prepares leaders to train and facilitate with staff serving families*

---

## Section 4.1 — Family as Core to the Ecosystem

- [ ] Frame parents and caregivers as essential partners in the youth development ecosystem, not obstacles or afterthoughts
- [ ] Apply Search Institute asset #1 (Family Support) and asset #2 (Positive Family Communication) as central goals of family engagement
- [ ] Explore how Pittman's ecosystem model includes family as the most proximal and influential system
- [ ] Acknowledge the diversity of family structures and cultural approaches to child-rearing
- [ ] Identify barriers families face to engagement (work schedules, language, prior negative experiences with institutions, distrust)

## Section 4.2 — Building Trust with Families

- [ ] Develop a family welcome and orientation process
- [ ] Create a family communication plan: frequency, channels, language accessibility, tone
- [ ] Practice asset-based communication with families — lead with strengths, not deficits
- [ ] Train staff and volunteers on how to listen to and validate caregiver concerns
- [ ] Develop a process for responding to family complaints or concerns with transparency and care
- [ ] Apply "moving at the speed of trust" (Emergent Strategy) — trust takes time and consistency

## Section 4.3 — Family Engagement Structures

- [ ] Establish regular family touchpoints (orientation night, family dinners, update calls)
- [ ] Create opportunities for families to give input on programming
- [ ] Develop a parent/caregiver advisory or leadership group
- [ ] Create family-to-family peer support opportunities
- [ ] Provide families with resources and connections to support their own well-being (not just their child's)
- [ ] Recognize and honor the wisdom caregivers hold about their own children

## Section 4.4 — Mentoring-Specific Family Engagement (EEPM Alignment)

- [ ] Obtain informed consent from families for mentoring participation
- [ ] Involve families appropriately in the matching process
- [ ] Set clear expectations with families about the mentoring relationship (mentor's role, boundaries, contact protocols)
- [ ] Maintain regular communication with families about match activities and progress
- [ ] Train families on how to support the mentoring relationship without overstepping
- [ ] Include families in match closure processes

## Section 4.5 — Culturally Responsive Family Engagement

- [ ] Assess the cultural, linguistic, and socioeconomic diversity of families you serve
- [ ] Develop engagement strategies that reflect and honor family culture
- [ ] Provide translation and interpretation services as needed
- [ ] Ensure materials are accessible in terms of literacy level and language
- [ ] Acknowledge and address historical reasons some communities distrust institutions
- [ ] Hire and partner with staff and volunteers who reflect the communities you serve

# MODULE 5: COMMUNITY STAKEHOLDER ENGAGEMENT

*For Organizational Leaders | Prepares leaders to engage partners and systems*

---

## Section 5.1 — Understanding the Community Ecosystem (Pittman Framework)

- [ ] Map the full youth development ecosystem in your community:
  - [ ] Schools and educational institutions
  - [ ] Faith communities
  - [ ] Youth-serving nonprofits
  - [ ] Parks and recreation
  - [ ] Health and mental health providers
  - [ ] Employers and workforce development organizations
  - [ ] Government and public systems (child welfare, juvenile justice, housing)
  - [ ] Civic and community organizations
  - [ ] Cultural and arts institutions
- [ ] Identify ecosystem strengths, gaps, and redundancies
- [ ] Identify specific assets or resources each stakeholder can contribute
- [ ] Assess power dynamics among stakeholders — who holds resources and who is often excluded?

## Section 5.2 — Building Authentic Community Partnerships

- [ ] Apply Emergent Strategy principles to partnership development: lead with relationships, not transactions
- [ ] Develop a partnership cultivation process: identify, engage, align, formalize, sustain
- [ ] Create a written MOU or partnership agreement that clarifies roles, expectations, and mutual accountability
- [ ] Practice asking: "What do we offer this partner?" before "What can this partner give us?"
- [ ] Identify community members and organizations with deep cultural trust and co-develop strategies with them
- [ ] Develop a stakeholder communication and engagement calendar

## Section 5.3 — Community Stakeholders as Asset Builders

- [ ] Connect stakeholders to Search Institute's developmental assets — identify how each partner can build specific assets
- [ ] Develop joint programming opportunities that multiply asset-building across settings
- [ ] Create a shared language across partners rooted in PYD principles
- [ ] Establish cross-sector referral pathways (e.g., youth identified in your program who need mental health services can access a partner's services)
- [ ] Document and communicate the asset-building work of community partners to youth and families

## Section 5.4 — Collective Impact & Systems Change

- [ ] Articulate the difference between direct service and systems change
- [ ] Identify systemic barriers that limit youth access to development opportunities (policy, funding, racism, poverty)
- [ ] Engage stakeholders in collective advocacy for youth-friendly systems and policies
- [ ] Apply Emergent Strategy's decentralization principle: leadership for change should be distributed across the community
- [ ] Develop mechanisms for community stakeholders to hold your organization accountable to its stated values
- [ ] Document and share learning across the ecosystem — your successes and failures help everyone improve

## Section 5.5 — Community-Based Mentoring Recruitment & Support

- [ ] Engage community stakeholders as volunteer recruitment pipelines
- [ ] Train community champions who can speak to the value of mentoring in trusted community spaces
- [ ] Identify and engage employer partners who can support employees as mentors
- [ ] Build community awareness campaigns around mentoring and youth development
- [ ] Create pathways for community members to connect to your program even if they can't formally mentor (group mentors, event volunteers, workshop facilitators)

# MODULE 6: PROGRAM DESIGN, EVALUATION & CONTINUOUS IMPROVEMENT

*For Organizational Leaders | Infrastructure to sustain quality programming*

---

## Section 6.1 — Designing Quality Youth Development Programs

- [ ] Use a logic model to connect program activities to intended outcomes
- [ ] Ensure program design is grounded in at least one evidence-based or evidence-informed framework
- [ ] Design programming to build developmental assets across multiple categories
- [ ] Build in age-appropriate youth voice in ongoing program design and feedback
- [ ] Ensure programming is culturally responsive and reflects the community served
- [ ] Review program design against EEPM standards (if mentoring is a component)

## Section 6.2 — Data, Evaluation & Learning (Emergent Strategy Integration)

- [ ] Define what "success" looks like — for youth, for families, for volunteers, for the community
- [ ] Develop a theory of change tied to PYD and developmental asset outcomes
- [ ] Identify qualitative and quantitative measures of success
- [ ] Create simple, non-burdensome data collection tools (surveys, observation checklists, check-ins)
- [ ] Establish a regular learning cycle: collect data, review data, adapt programming based on data
- [ ] Apply Emergent Strategy: failure is information — create a culture where honest reflection is welcomed
- [ ] Share findings with youth, families, volunteers, and community stakeholders (not just funders)

## Section 6.3 — Staff & Volunteer Sustainability

- [ ] Apply the fractal principle: healthy staff culture produces healthy youth culture
- [ ] Develop a staff wellness and professional development plan
- [ ] Build a volunteer recognition and appreciation cycle
- [ ] Create clear pathways for staff and volunteers to grow within your organization
- [ ] Conduct regular team reflections: what's working, what's not, what needs to shift
- [ ] Practice adaptive leadership — be willing to change course when something isn't working

## Section 6.4 — Organizational Equity Audit

- [ ] Review organizational leadership, staff, and volunteer composition against the demographics of youth served
- [ ] Assess whether youth, families, and community members from marginalized backgrounds have real power in your organization
- [ ] Identify and address any discriminatory policies, practices, or culture patterns
- [ ] Develop an ongoing equity commitment — not a one-time statement, but an embedded practice
- [ ] Use Emergent Strategy's principle of interdependence: equity work is not separate from youth development work — it is the same work
